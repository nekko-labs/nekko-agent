# Driving your home model from your phone (remote control)

The user opens **Settings → Remote access**, picks how the phone connects (`[changed 2026-10-23]`, see "Two ways to connect" below), and taps **Pair a device**: a QR appears carrying a **single-use pairing code that expires in 10 minutes**. They scan it from the phone (the Nekko Agent app, the phone's camera app, which opens the Nekko Agent app, or any browser) and the phone shows up under **Paired devices** with a live connection dot. A phone browser gets the app against the home machine, limited to what a paired phone may do (`[updated 2026-10-14]`, see "What a paired phone may do"): chats, approvals and questions work; training runs, goals, the Agent board, terminals and most settings are refused by the computer. The native app is the focused version of the same thing (next section). Inference and tool calls execute locally under the machine's guardrails/sandbox.

Security is layered: the local agent dials out to the relay (no inbound ports), every payload is end-to-end encrypted (the relay routes only ciphertext, so relayed local-model use is inherently zero-data-retention), and on top of the transport every device must pass a handshake against the machine's **device registry**: unknown devices are denied unless they present a live pairing code, each device can be **renamed or revoked** (revocation kicks it mid-connection), and **Rotate secret** is the cryptographic kill switch that unpairs everything. `[changed 2026-10-16]` A revoked device is re-admitted only with a live one-time pairing code, the same proof a new device needs; without one it is still denied `revoked`. Before this, a phone (whose device id survives "forget this computer") could never pair again short of Rotate secret, while the phone told the user to "pair it again" (found on the first real-phone test). The Android app now names the steps (Pair a device on the computer, scan the new QR) and keeps Rotate secret as the fallback for computers without this change. Same rule in `packages/shared/src/remote.ts` and `crates/nekko-remote/src/hello.rs`, held equal by the golden verdicts. Remote access survives restarts; the agent reconnects on boot. Full guide: [docs/REMOTE.md](docs/REMOTE.md).

**Relay on this computer (Tailscale first).** `[implemented 2026-10-16]` For a phone and computer on the same [Tailscale](https://tailscale.com) network, the Remote access card offers **Relay on this computer** → **Start relay**. The desktop runs the relay itself, bound only to this computer's Tailscale address, and shows the address phones use: the MagicDNS name, or the `100.x` address when the Tailscale CLI isn't there. **Use this relay** fills the relay field. Nothing is hosted and nothing is reachable from outside the tailnet. WireGuard carries the hop, and the end-to-end layer is unchanged on top.
- The switch survives restarts.
- With Tailscale off the card says so, links to Tailscale, and starts the relay once Tailscale connects. It never runs without Tailscale and never binds the internet.
- **It is on the LAN too** `[implemented 2026-10-19, TASKS IO-20; unit-tested, golden parity TS/Rust; not yet run on a real Wi-Fi with a phone]`. Next to the tailnet address it listens on this computer's private LAN addresses, RFC 1918 only (10/8, 172.16/12, 192.168/16): never `0.0.0.0`, link-local or a public address, and not the adapters only VMs and containers sit behind (WSL, Hyper-V, Docker, VirtualBox, VMware, bridges), whose private addresses aren't the phone's Wi-Fi. An address that can't be bound is skipped rather than failing the relay. It is one process with one room table, so the host and the Rust daemon still hold one room. On by default when the relay runs, with an **Also on this Wi-Fi** switch on the card (saved as `"lan": false` in `local-relay.json` only when off; desktop-only `localRelay:setLan`). It follows changed LAN addresses as it follows the tailnet one. While remote access uses this relay, pairing links carry each LAN address as an extra `alt=ws://192.168.x.y:4400` route (below). Chosen by Philip as the second route instead of the hosted relay.
- It follows a changed tailnet address.
- It explains failures, such as the port already in use.
- It has no push keys, so phones get no "finished" pushes through it; the managed relay keeps those.
- Enable refuses a plain `ws://` relay on the open internet, with the same rule the phones apply (TASKS PH1).
- **Pairing links with more than one route** `[implemented 2026-10-19, TASKS IO-20]`. A link may repeat `alt=<relay url>` after `relay=`: other addresses of the same relay. Each must pass the PH1 rule (`relayUrlProblem`); one that doesn't is dropped, never the whole link. Trailing slashes are trimmed, repeats and the relay itself are dropped, at most four are kept. Links without `alt` are the old links and parse as before. One parser per client, held together by one fixture written from `packages/shared/src/pairing-link.ts`: the iPhone's `Pairing.swift`, Android's `Pairing.kt` (Android reads `alt` but does not dial it yet). Phones paired before this keep one route until they scan a new code.
- **Two ways to connect** `[implemented 2026-10-23; unit-tested and checked in a hidden synthetic renderer (light and dark, 900 and 420 wide); not yet in the installed app or the Local Web UI]`. Before remote access is on, the card shows two side-by-side options and nothing else: **Tailscale** (badged Free: the phone connects straight to this computer, needs the Tailscale app on both; shows the tailnet name when connected, otherwise a Get Tailscale link and the button stays off) and **Nekko Cloud** (works anywhere, nothing to install, end-to-end encrypted). **Use Tailscale** starts this computer's relay and turns remote access on through it; **Use Nekko Cloud** turns it on through the managed relay. The owner always sees "Nekko Cloud", never the relay or its URL: once on, the card says *Connected through: Nekko Cloud* (or *Tailscale*, or the URL of their own relay). A self-hosted relay URL sits behind a collapsed **Use your own relay**. The Relay on this computer card (with the LAN switch) shows only while Tailscale is the connection. Where this computer cannot run a relay (the Local Web UI) only Nekko Cloud is offered. This replaces the prefilled relay field, its Tailscale default below and the Enable button. Asked for by Philip: two very simple choices, Tailscale free but needing an external app, or Nekko Cloud.
- **It is the default when Tailscale is here** `[implemented 2026-10-19, TASKS IO-19 TS-1; superseded 2026-10-23 by Two ways to connect]`. With Tailscale connected on this computer and no relay chosen, the relay field starts with this computer's relay address instead of the managed URL, and a line under it says so. **Enable** starts the relay first and then turns remote access on with the address it ended up on. If the relay can't start, Enable says why and stays off. A relay the owner typed or picked is kept. A saved managed URL counts as no choice, because it was only ever the default. The MagicDNS name is looked up as soon as Tailscale is seen, so the default is the name and not the `100.x` address. Android can only dial the name. Without Tailscale the managed relay stays the default.

## Finding the computer and choosing the transport (decided 2026-10-12)

The QR stays the universal pairing path, but on a home network it should rarely be needed. The desktop **advertises itself** with mDNS / DNS-SD (Bonjour on macOS and iOS, NSD on Android, an mDNS responder in the Windows and Linux builds) as `_nekko-agent._tcp`, carrying only a display name, the relay URL and a pairing-session hint, never a secret. The phone's Computers tab lists advertised computers on the same Wi-Fi and, when Tailscale is on, on the tailnet (unicast DNS-SD over the tailnet, the same mechanism OpenClaw uses), so pairing becomes "tap the computer, approve on the desktop". The single-use pairing code and the device registry handshake are unchanged; discovery only removes the paste.

**Visible on this network (desktop advertisement).** `[implemented 2026-10-17; advertisement only, off by default]` With remote access on, the Remote access card has a **Visible on this network** switch. Switched on, the desktop answers mDNS / DNS-SD queries for `_nekko-agent._tcp.local` itself: a small responder on plain `dgram` multicast (224.0.0.251:5353, every LAN interface), with no native module and the same code on macOS, Windows and Linux. It announces twice on start and whenever the name, LAN addresses or relay change, answers multicast, QU and legacy-unicast questions, and sends a goodbye (TTL 0) when switched off or on quit. The TXT record carries only `v=1`, `name` (the computer's name) and, when **Relay on this computer** is running, `relay` (its tailnet URL, also the SRV port; otherwise the SRV port is 0). It never carries the room, the key or a pairing code, so a listing alone can't get a phone in. Loopback and tailnet addresses are left out of the A records (the tailnet has no multicast). Without a LAN address it waits and says so. The switch is kept in `network-visibility.json`; the channels are desktop-only `networkVisibility:*`, never phone-callable. Both phones list it under *On this network* on their Pair screens (below). **Not yet:** the `pair=1` / `lobby` hint and pairing from a listing, which wait on the pairing decision in [reports/phone-discovery-pairing-proposal.md](reports/phone-discovery-pairing-proposal.md); whether the switch defaults to on while remote access is on is part of that decision.

Transport policy follows one rule, copied from the strictest competitor: **plain `ws://` is accepted only toward private ranges** (loopback, RFC 1918, link-local, and Tailscale's `100.64.0.0/10` CGNAT range), and the desktop refuses to generate a pairing link with a plaintext URL that points anywhere else. Everything public is `wss://`. The end-to-end layer applies in all cases; the rule protects the transport-auth query string and keeps a misconfigured relay from leaking metadata. The desktop can run the relay itself for the Tailscale case ("Relay on this computer"), which is the free, nothing-hosted default; a hosted relay is for phones that are not on the user's network.

## Hosted relay tiers, free notifications and what the server may know (decided 2026-10-12)

The hosted relay (nekkoagent.com, or a self-hosted copy of the same image) has two tiers plus the self-hosted option, in the spirit of OpenClaw's free push relay and Happy Coder's free sync server, but with the traffic leg metered so it never has to be switched off:

- **Push is free for everyone.** Content-free "your run finished / needs you" notifications through the hosted relay cost nothing, including for users whose traffic rides Tailscale or their own relay: the desktop registers for push only, sends the content-free `notify` frame, and the relay forwards it to APNs / FCM. No account needed; the device token is the only state.
- **Free traffic tier, limited and fair-queued.** Without a Cloud account the hosted relay accepts a paired room with a request budget per device per day (a tunable; start around a few hundred requests and a frame-byte cap sized for chat, not file sync), one computer and two devices per room, and a **fair-share scheduler**: when the machine is busy, free rooms are throttled first and evenly (token bucket per room refilled from a shared pool), paid rooms keep their rate. Hitting a limit degrades gracefully: the phone shows "free relay busy / daily budget used, resumes at …" and offers Tailscale or Cloud; nothing is silently dropped mid-run. Limits are enforced per room and device, never by reading content.
- **Cloud (paid, or run your own).** A Cloud account lifts the budget and device limits, keeps rate under load, enables several computers per account and the ZDR / history features already specified. The same image, with `NEKKO_RELAY_ALLOW_UNAUTHENTICATED=1` or an authz URL of their own, gives self-hosters the paid behaviour for free, forever. The OSS app still never license-checks; the relay answers with the plan's allowance and the app just displays it.

**Server analytics, PII-free by construction.** The hosted relay keeps enough to run the service and to learn where it is used and for what, and nothing that identifies a person or a conversation. What is counted: connections, rooms and devices per day, app platform and version, desktop OS and version, coarse geography from the connecting IP (country or region, resolved at the edge and then discarded, never the IP), relay-side event names that are already content-free (run finished, approval needed, question asked, push sent), rate-limit hits and tier, and the opaque `kind` of a session as the desktop volunteers it (chat, task, training, goal) if the user leaves the **"Share anonymous usage"** switch on. Never kept: IPs, device tokens beyond their push purpose, room codes in analytics, message bodies, prompts, model names tied to a user, file names, folder paths, timestamps finer than the hour tied to a device. Device and room identifiers are hashed with a daily rotating salt before they reach the analytics store, so a day's active-device count is possible and a cross-day trail is not. Analytics are aggregated on the relay and shipped as counters, not rows; the raw connection log rotates within 24 hours. The desktop and phone show exactly what is sent, with one switch to turn it off, and turning it off changes no feature. This is the whole list, and the website states it in the same words.
